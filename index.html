Equations and constants

The carbonate system is two equilibria over one conserved total:

CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ K₁ = {H⁺}{HCO₃⁻} / {CO₂} HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ K₂ = {H⁺}{CO₃²⁻} / {HCO₃⁻} C_T = [CO₂*] + [HCO₃⁻] + [CO₃²⁻]

With h = 10^(−pH) and conditional constants K₁′, K₂′ the fractions are closed-form, so no iteration on pH is needed:

α₀ = 1 / (1 + K₁′/h + K₁′K₂′/h²) → CO₂(aq) α₁ = 1 / (h/K₁′ + 1 + K₂′/h) → HCO₃⁻ α₂ = 1 / (h²/(K₁′K₂′) + h/K₂′ + 1) → CO₃²⁻

Model A — thermodynamic constants plus activity corrections. K₁, K₂, KH, Kw and Ksp follow Plummer & Busenberg (1982), valid 0–90 °C. Because a glass electrode reads hydrogen ion activity, the constants are made conditional on concentration through the activity coefficients:

K₁′ = K₁ · γ(CO₂) / γ(HCO₃⁻) K₂′ = K₂ · γ(HCO₃⁻) / γ(CO₃²⁻)

γ for charged species comes from Davies, extended Debye–Hückel (Truesdell–Jones, WATEQ parameters) or the limiting law. Neutral CO₂ is salted out by the Setchenow relation log γ = k_s·I. Debye–Hückel A and B are computed at the sample temperature from the dielectric constant of water.

Model B — saline stoichiometric constants. pK₁* and pK₂* from Millero's fits to potentiometric seawater data, of the form pKᵢ* = pKᵢ⁰ + Aᵢ + Bᵢ/T + Cᵢ·ln T with A, B, C polynomials in √S. These are concentration constants on the seawater pH scale, so no activity coefficients are applied — the ionic interactions are already inside the fitted constant. Check values at S = 35, 25 °C: pK₁* = 5.840, pK₂* = 8.964. K₀ from Weiss (1974); K*sp for calcite and aragonite from Mucci (1983).

Saturation index is SI = log₁₀(IAP/K_sp) with IAP = {Ca²⁺}{CO₃²⁻} in model A and [Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻] in model B. LSI is reported as pH − pH_s for the same system. SI > 0 means calcium carbonate is thermodynamically favoured to precipitate; it says nothing about kinetics or induction time.

Reading a Shimadzu IC result into this tool

On a Shimadzu TOC-L or TOC-V, the IC channel acidifies the sample with phosphoric acid and sparges it, converting every inorganic carbon species to CO₂ for NDIR detection. Some configurations use HCl to reach pH < 3 instead. Either way the measurement is a total: dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate and carbonate all report as one number in mg C/L. That number is exactly the C_T this calculator splits, so enter it with the mg C/L basis and no conversion.

  • Calibration basis. The IC channel is normally calibrated with sodium bicarbonate, which is a carbon standard — the reported value is carbon mass, not HCO₃⁻ mass. Using the mg/L as HCO₃⁻ basis here would understate C_T by a factor of about five.
  • Sampling is the dominant error, not the instrument. The split depends on the pH the water had in situ. Sample with no headspace, keep it cold, measure pH and temperature at the same moment as the sample is drawn, and analyse quickly. A degassed or air-equilibrated sample loses free CO₂, which lowers C_T and raises pH, and the tool will then report a carbonate-shifted split that the process never had.
  • pH measured at a different temperature than the process. Enter the process temperature and the pH as read; then re-run at the measurement temperature to see the sensitivity. Below about pH 8 the split is not very temperature sensitive; above it, pK₂ moves the CO₃²⁻ fraction noticeably.
  • High-salt samples. The high-salt combustion kit and the salt-tolerance of the IC channel affect the TC side far more than IC. For brines the larger correction is chemical, not analytical — use the saline model or at least a realistic ionic strength.
  • Cross-check. If you also have a titrated alkalinity, compare it with the carbonate alkalinity reported here. Agreement within a few percent means pH, TIC and temperature are mutually consistent. A large gap usually means non-carbonate alkalinity (ammonia, borate, organic acids, silicate) or a degassed sample.
Validity limits and what this tool does not do
  • Davies is dependable to about I = 0.5 mol/L and degrades beyond it. Past roughly I = 0.7 the honest options are the saline model, if the ionic ratios are broadly seawater-like, or a Pitzer-based code such as PHREEQC with the pitzer database. This page warns but does not stop you.
  • The saline model is fitted for S = 0–50 and 0–50 °C in seawater-proportion matrices. A sodium chloride brine or an RO concentrate at the same salinity is not the same matrix, and extrapolating it is a judgement call.
  • Mixing pH scales is the commonest silent error. A meter calibrated with NBS buffers in a saline sample is not on the seawater scale. The offset is approximately 0.10–0.15 pH units at seawater salinity and it propagates directly into the CO₂ and CO₃²⁻ fractions.
  • No ion pairing is modelled. In hard or high-sulfate waters a real fraction of carbonate exists as CaCO₃⁰, CaHCO₃⁺, MgCO₃⁰ and NaCO₃⁻, so free CO₃²⁻ is lower than reported and calcite SI is correspondingly optimistic.
  • Alkalinity here is the carbonate and water contribution only. Ammonia, borate, silicate, phosphate, sulfide and organic acids are not included.
  • Pressure is atmospheric. No correction for depth or for pressurised membrane feed channels.
